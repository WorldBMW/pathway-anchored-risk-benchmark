## SUMMARY

Spatial-transcriptomic models on a few dozen donors can fit labels by memorizing donor identity, not disease state. Detection is now possible, but leaves no remedy: the obvious post-hoc fix, projecting out donor-mean directions, destroys the transferable disease signal with the leak. We introduce MRP-ST-Repair, a training-time patient-adversarial repair method with a theory of signal-preserving repair. Repair keeps shared disease programs in 75/75 positive-control runs across amplitudes, doses, seeds, and backbones, while the detector stays non-blind in 45/45 confounding-control runs. A proposition shows that a naive detect-then-accept dual gate is feasible only below a pre-repair association-to-null-scale ratio τ_base/σ̂ < 2.35, so we replace it with a three-axis acceptance protocol. Repairability depends on label–identity coupling: causal-type labels (etiological HPV status) retain a transferable fraction 0.38–0.59 at accepted operating points, whereas ordinal severity admits none. A donor-count feasibility boundary, a sibling-slice inflation effect, and survival of a keratinization program complete the method.

**Keywords:** spatial transcriptomics; information leakage; adversarial training; model auditing; transferability; head and neck cancer; HPV

## INTRODUCTION

Deep models for spatial transcriptomics (ST) learn spot- and slide-level representations that drive clustering, deconvolution, spatial-domain detection, and outcome prediction. The cohorts available for these tasks are structurally thin: Visium slides are expensive, and a typical study carries a handful to a few dozen patients. When a patient contributes multiple spots — or multiple slides — any model trained on spot-level splits can succeed by recognizing *which patient* a spot came from rather than *what disease state* it represents. This failure mode is now measurable: in a companion study[1] we introduced a calibrated label-dependence index (LDI) that detects such patient-block leakage under permutation inference, and showed that models fitting severity labels almost perfectly within a cervical neoplasia cohort (in-sample AUROC 1.00) collapse to chance under leave-one-donor-out (LODO) evaluation (AUROC 0.497).

Detection, however, is only half of the problem. The question this paper addresses is constructive: *given that a trained ST model is leaking, how do we remove the leak, and how do we keep the disease signal the model actually learned?* The naive answer — cleaning the embedding after training — does not work, as we show below: after training, donor identity and disease signal are folded into the same directions, and any post-hoc filter that removes one removes the other. Repair must therefore be built into training, and it must be paired with a criterion that certifies both that the leak is gone and that the signal survived.

The nearest prior work each solves a different problem. Batch- and patient-robust contrastive encoders (e.g., arXiv:2503.07173)[2] produce cross-donor-consistent representations but provide no leakage metric, no calibrated acceptance criterion, and no operating characteristics; group-robustness objectives (Group-DRO,[3] invariant risk minimization[4]) suppress spurious correlations without any instrument to verify that label dependence was actually removed; batch-correction methods (Harmony,[5] scVI-style conditional models,[6] spatial-alignment tools) align distributions across slides, which is orthogonal to removing label dependence nested within patients; and donor-disjoint splitting is a preventive practice, not a remediation of an already-trained model. In the machine-learning literature beyond genomics, contamination-tracing pipelines that detect test-set leakage through temporal train–test partitioning (language-model evaluation)[7] share the spirit but neither the spatial structure nor the biological endpoints of our setting.

This paper contributes **MRP-ST-Repair**, a training-time method for signal-preserving de-identification of ST models, together with three findings we believe generalize beyond ST:

1. **A training-time repair operator that provably does not destroy shared signal (C1).** A patient-adversarial operator family is trained with the encoder, and the *same calibrated instrument* used for detection is repurposed as an *acceptance* referee — yielding measured operating characteristics for both. Across 75 positive-control runs the shared disease program survives every dose, while the detector stays non-blind on 45 identity-confounding runs.
2. **A statistical theory separating detection from acceptance (Proposition 1).** We prove that a "detect-then-accept" dual gate is feasible only when the pre-repair label association is small relative to the null scale — so the very statistic that certifies detection (a large LDI) can make a naive acceptance gate structurally infeasible — and we introduce a three-axis acceptance protocol that replaces the conflation.
3. **A quantitative characterization of when repair preserves signal.** Across simulated ground-truth cohorts and two real cancer cohorts, repairability is governed by cohort structure: the label–identity coupling type (etiological versus ordinal) sets the retention budget, and a donor-count feasibility boundary (n\* ≈ 16–32 donors at our reference signal, shifting rightward as signal weakens) sets the structural precondition. A "sibling-slice inflation" effect further shows that slice-level cross-validation systematically overestimates generalization in multi-slide cohorts, and must be replaced by donor-level holdout.

What survives repair is also, reassuringly, real biology: in an external head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cohort, the HPV-status signal that persists through accepted repair maps onto the canonical squamous-differentiation/keratinization program.

## RESULTS

### A training-time patient-adversarial repair operator that preserves shared signal

**Why repair must be imposed during training.** The cheapest repair for an already-trained model is a post-hoc projection that removes the patient-mean subspace from the embedding (identity residualization). We tested this first because it requires no retraining, on the cervical cohort and on the simulation arms, and it fails mechanistically. On the cervical cohort, projecting out a single patient-mean direction (k = 1) left in-sample label association intact (τ_in unchanged at 0.46) while the leave-one-out (LOO) association — the part that transfers across patients — was destroyed (mean τ_LOO from 0.46 to −0.08; per-seed −0.38 to +0.19); pushing the projection to k = 2 collapsed both together (τ_LOO −0.44, τ_in 0.05). On the shared-program simulation — whose disease program is a *shared, donor-transferable* signal of known amplitude (Experimental Procedures) — the collapse was immediate and total (τ_LOO 0.71 → −0.71, τ_in → 0.02 at k = 1). In other words, the projection deleted *both* the patient-specific nuisance fingerprints and the generalizable disease information, because after training they occupy the same subspace (Figure 1B): the model has already folded patient identity and disease signal into joint directions that a post-hoc linear filter cannot disentangle. The in-sample metric, which only measures memorization capacity, stays high while the transferable signal is already gone, and gives no warning. This negative result sets the design space for everything that follows: the encoder must be shaped, during training, to keep patient-identity directions out of the representation in the first place. It is the reason MRP-ST-Repair is a training-time method.

**Repair operator (Method B).** We train a graph-attention encoder[8] on the spot-level spatial graph (k-nearest-neighbor, k = 6, with a large coordinate offset that prevents cross-slide edges) with a linear decoder reconstructing expression, and we attach a patient-classification adversary through a gradient-reversal layer (GRL)[9,10] with a ramped reversal strength α = min(1, 2·epoch/T) (the stability-hardened variant ramps only after a 50-epoch adversary warm-up, with α = 0 before it). The adversary maximizes patient-identity accuracy; the encoder, through the reversal, minimizes it. A single hyperparameter λ scales the adversarial term: λ = 0 recovers the un-repaired baseline, and increasing λ doses the repair.

**Safety: a strong shared program survives every dose.** A method that claims to "remove leakage" must first prove it does not remove *everything*. We ran a safety panel on the shared-program positive-control cohort across program amplitudes {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.5}, adversarial doses λ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5}, three seeds with the GAT encoder (60 runs), plus the GCN encoder at the reference amplitude (15 runs). In **75 of 75 runs**, the post-repair LOO association stayed at the analytic ceiling for the cohort's class sizes (τ = 0.707 for 50-vs-40 donors — the maximum Kendall τ attainable for binary labels of those multiplicities, derived in Experimental Procedures; the transferable fraction R ≥ 0.989 in every run), and the LDI stayed at its high baseline (≈ 5.8 at the reference amplitude; 5.6–6.1 across all amplitudes) at every dose (Figure 2A). This is the correct outcome, not a failure: a shared, donor-transferable program *should* survive de-identification, and the detector *should* keep flagging it as genuine label dependence. Repair does not extinguish real signal; it extinguishes patient identity.

**Non-blindness: the detector still separates true from spurious after repair.** On the identity-confounding control cohort — where every donor, regardless of class, carries a donor-specific pathway fingerprint of identical amplitude, so labels can only be fit by memorizing identity — the repaired models were correctly graded unsupported in 45 of 45 runs across configurations (GAT, GCN, and a stability-hardened variant; Figure 2B): LDI never crossed the 1.645 gate. The instrument's false-alarm behavior is therefore preserved under repair. On the weak-signal-plus-heterogeneity cohort, where only one of three seeds was detectable at baseline, moderate repair (λ = 0.5) raised the association in all three seeds (mean τ rising from 0.05 to 0.145, with the two previously undetectable seeds moving from −0.03 to +0.13) though none crossed the calibrated gate (per-seed LDI 1.08–1.39; Table S1). This is a small but consistent de-identification-as-preconditioning effect; we report its magnitude honestly and do not build claims on it.

**Backbone generality.** Replacing the GAT encoder with a graph-convolutional encoder[11] reproduced the safety panel (15/15 runs at ceiling) and the non-blindness result (15/15 no false alarms) exactly, and reproduced the external-cohort dose–response shape (Table S5). The repair mechanism is not an artifact of attention.

### A statistical condition separating detection from acceptance (Proposition 1)

A repair operator is only usable with a criterion that certifies both removal of the leak and preservation of the signal. Accepting a repair naively seems to require two conditions: the label dependence should be gone (LDI < 1.645, its calibrated detection gate), and the transferable signal should be preserved (τ ≥ c·τ_base, with c = 0.7 by protocol). Proposition 1 shows these conditions conflict in a way that is *predictable from pre-repair quantities*.

> **Proposition 1 (feasibility of the dual acceptance gate).** Let LDI(τ) = (τ − τ⊥)/σ̂ be the calibrated index, strictly increasing in τ, with gate value 1.645 and null floor τ⊥ ≈ 0. The post-repair τ feasible set of the dual gate {LDI < 1.645 ∧ τ ≥ c·τ_base} is non-empty if and only if c·τ_base < τ⊥ + 1.645·σ̂, i.e. **τ_base/σ̂ < 1.645/c + τ⊥/(c·σ̂) ≈ 2.35** for c = 0.7.
>
> **Corollary 1.** For any cohort with a fixed true baseline association τ_base > 0, the dual gate is structurally empty once the cohort is large enough (σ̂ → 0 as the donor count grows), because the gate then demands near-zero τ while preservation demands a large fraction of τ_base. The dual gate therefore conflates "leakage removed" with "signal removed" exactly when the signal is strongest.

*Proof.* Immediate from monotonicity: LDI < 1.645 ⟺ τ < τ⊥ + 1.645σ̂; intersecting with [c·τ_base, τ_base] is non-empty iff c·τ_base < τ⊥ + 1.645σ̂. ∎

The proposition is not a curiosity; it predicted our experimental outcomes exactly (Table 1). The shared-program simulation has τ_base/σ̂ = 5.8 and the cervical cohort ≈ 2.4 — both above the ≈ 2.35 threshold — and indeed **no** dose of repair ever passed the dual gate there, by construction rather than by failure. The external HNSCC cohort sits at τ_base/σ̂ ≈ 2.2, just below threshold, and is the only one of the three in which an acceptance window exists — and where accepted operating points were in fact observed. A method designer who did not know this algebra would have concluded, wrongly, that repair "failed" on every strong-signal cohort.

We therefore replace the dual gate with a **three-axis acceptance protocol**: (i) **safety** — the transferable association τ_LOO is preserved relative to baseline on positive controls; (ii) **leakage reduction** — the in-sample memorization gap (τ_in − τ_LOO) shrinks, and on identity-confounding controls the fingerprint contribution is reduced; (iii) **non-blindness** — the instrument still grades known controls correctly after repair (true program: supported; identity-confounding: unsupported). Acceptance is then a statement about *which kind* of label dependence remains, not a demand that all label dependence vanish.

One quantitative honesty note belongs here. On the identity-confounding control, the in-sample fingerprint clearance under repair was partial (≈ 12% reduction at the strongest dose, stable across seeds and across the stability-hardened variant; Table S2): memorized identity rides not only on the pathway fingerprint we planted but also on unplanned donor-specific components (batch offsets, spatial-field phase). This is a useful calibration of axis (ii) — in-sample τ is a *memorization-capacity bound*, not a calibrated leakage measure, which is precisely why the LOO-based LDI, not τ_in, is the referee.

### Repairability is a property of label–identity coupling, not of the method

Two real cohorts, two label types, one method, opposite outcomes.

**Cervical neoplasia (ordinal severity; 35 Visium slides).** With unbounded identity information, the baseline model's LOO association is large relative to the null scale (τ_LOO ≈ 0.42; LDI ≈ 2.4–2.5 at λ = 0; Table S3). Dosing the repair produced a bistable response that survived stability hardening (adversary warm-up, gradient clipping, spectral-normalized adversary heads): low doses (λ ≤ 1) left LDI above the gate (2.00–2.52), while at high doses (λ ∈ {2, 5}) every seed produced below-gate runs (LDI 0.62–1.15) that retained only 27–50% of the transferable association, and the response was erratic rather than monotone — the same seed sat above the gate at one high dose and below it at the other (e.g., LDI 2.41 at λ = 2 but 0.80 at λ = 5 in one seed, 1.15 at λ = 2 but 2.42 at λ = 5 in another). There is no stable intermediate operating point. The mechanistic reading is direct: an ordinal severity label — normal, high-grade intraepithelial lesion, carcinoma — encodes each patient's individual pathological history; severity and identity are nearly the same variable in a small cohort, so any dose strong enough to strip identity also strips the label. Consistent with this, the companion study found LODO AUROC collapse from 1.00 to 0.497 on the same structure.

**HNSCC, external validation (etiological binary label; 16 patients, GSE268014).** We downloaded a public Visium cohort of HPV-positive and HPV-negative head-and-neck tumors and trained the same repair stack. Throughout, we quantify repair outcomes by the transferable-fraction statistic R = τ_LOO/τ_in — the share of what a run fits in-sample that actually transfers across donors (R = 1 at the analytic ceiling). The dose–response was qualitatively different and, for the first time in a real cohort, complete and non-monotonic in both statistics (Table 2; Figure 2C): the LDI fell with dose to a minimum near λ = 0.5 (1.13–1.37, three of three seeds under the gate), then partially rebounded; R fell and then recovered, with one seed at the maximal dose regaining a transferable fraction of R = 0.90 (τ_LOO = 0.64); in the independent non-hardened replication, the same seed and dose reached the analytic ceiling for 8-vs-8 patients (τ_LOO = τ_in = 0.7303 — the theoretical maximum), i.e. a point of *perfect donor-transferable generalization* (Table S4). Accepted operating points (LDI < 1.645) existed across λ ∈ {0.5, 1, 2} — seven of nine seed×dose cells, with all three seeds passing at λ = 0.5 — and the transferable fraction at those cells spanned R = 0.38–0.59 (the non-hardened replication reproduced the same shape, per-seed R 0.34–0.75 at λ = 2; Table S4). Permutation-depth sensitivity was nil: recomputing the null with B = 10,000 instead of 2,000 permutations flipped no gate decision in the 15 external-cohort runs and moved σ̂ by ≤ 4.4%, and the LOO statistic recomputed bit-identically.

**The contrast is the finding.** An etiological label — *which virus caused this tumor* — is external to the patient's identity: many patients share the same cause, and the molecular program it induces (cell-cycle entry, squamous differentiation) is genuinely shared. An ordinal severity label is internal to the patient's history. Under identical methods and dose schedules, the etiological label preserves a transferable fraction of 0.38–0.59 at accepted operating points (0.69–0.94 unrepaired; one maximal-dose seed reached 0.90, which the three-axis protocol scores as surviving shared signal rather than acceptance failure), while the ordinal label admits none. **Repairability is thus a measurable property of the cohort's label–identity coupling structure** — a design consideration for anyone planning a small-cohort ST study, and a decomposition of "external validity" into a quantity one can estimate before training.

### A donor-count feasibility boundary — and why the detector itself is donor-robust

If repairability depends on cohort structure, how does it scale with the number of patients? We generated a two-dimensional phase grid: donors n ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32, 64} (with the 5:4 class ratio fixed) at one slice per donor, a shared donor fingerprint as the sole leakage channel (amplitude matched to the positive controls), and repair dose λ on the third axis, at two signal strengths (1.0 and 0.3), three seeds per cell.

Two results, one negative and one positive, both informative. **Negative:** at λ = 0 (no repair), the LOO association stayed at ceiling for n ≥ 8 at signal 1.0 — detection is donor-robust at one-slice-per-donor, because the LOO probe already blocks the only leakage channel; a "leakage phase boundary" for detection does not exist at k = 1. (The detector's own robustness is a deliverable of this grid.) **Positive:** the *repair* columns exhibit a sharp boundary (Figure 3A). At signal 1.0, for n ≤ 16 the cell-mean transferable fraction collapsed under most doses (minima per donor count: −0.67 at n = 4, 0.08 at n = 8, 0.58 at n = 16; per-seed values in Table S8), while for n ≥ 32 no dose in any cell dropped the cell mean below 0.86 (and all cell means were ≥ 0.98 at n = 64): **the feasibility boundary lies between 16 and 32 donors** (five-seed verification at the boundary cells in Table S6). At signal 0.3, the boundary moved rightward, landing between 32 and 64 donors (worst cell mean 0.80 at n = 32). The mechanism is the subspace argument of the post-hoc failure, now at the population level: with few donors, the identity subspace spans (or contains) the program direction, so any dose that suppresses identity necessarily suppresses the program; with many donors, identity directions average out and the program survives. Weaker signals are geometrically closer to the identity subspace and need more donors to be recoverable. Dose–response within the damaged region was again non-monotonic (mid doses worst), matching the real-cohort behavior in the previous section.

This boundary is the quantitative form of claim C2: it gives a *precondition* for repair (a donor budget at a given signal strength) rather than a guarantee, and it explains *why* the cervical cohort (35 slides but effectively few information donors per severity stratum, with identity-severely-coupled labels) and the 16-patient HNSCC cohort (etiological label, n just below the signal-1.0 boundary but with weaker coupling) sit where they do on the repairability map.

### Sibling-slice inflation: slice-level cross-validation overestimates generalization in multi-slide cohorts

The phase grid also revealed a trap that affects every multi-slide ST study. We re-ran the slice axis with k ∈ {2, 4, 8} slices per donor (16 donors), and evaluated two statistics: the slice-level LOO τ used so far, and a **donor-level leave-one-donor-out** τ (τ_LDO), where every slice of a held-out donor is removed jointly. At λ = 0 the two coincide at the ceiling (0.713 for the 18-vs-14 slide classes) — the shared program transfers across donors, as it should. After repair, they separate dramatically (Figure 3C): slice-level LOO stayed pinned at the ceiling in all 45 runs across k ∈ {2, 4, 8} (each k at its own class-size ceiling), while τ_LDO collapsed (at k = 2: 0.713 → 0.585 at λ = 2 and → 0.230 at λ = 5). The slice-level split is silently rescued by **sibling slices**: the held-out slice's donor remains in training through its brother slices, so "generalization" is partly a re-identification match. The companion study's LODO AUROC collapse is exactly this effect in the wild; here it is produced and controlled with a single knob. Our repairability claims above are therefore computed at k = 1, where the two statistics coincide by construction, and we state the resulting recommendation as a protocol rule: **in multi-slide cohorts, model acceptance must be measured at the donor level, never the slice level.**

### What survives repair is known biology

If the repair keeps genuinely shared programs, the surviving representation should decode into recognizable biology. We took the λ = 2 seed-0 run from the external cohort, fit the HPV-status discriminant direction in the repaired embedding space by ridge regression on slice-mean representations, and mapped that direction back to gene space through the trained linear decoder (a strict pairing: the decoder belongs to the same run as the embedding; Figure 4). Ranking genes by |loading| in unique-symbol space (the 6,586-entry union panel contains 4,647 unique symbols), the top-100 genes were significantly enriched for the squamous-differentiation/keratinization program — KRT14, KRT16, and ID1 among the 11 in-panel HPV-positive reference markers, together with keratinization-family genes SPRR1B and SPRR3 (hypergeometric p = 0.0014, with 11 of the 15 reference markers present in the panel; empirical permutation p = 0.0015 against a null expectation of 0.23 marker hits per 100 genes) — with plasma-cell (IGKC, rank 2) and stromal (COL1A2, rank 17) components completing the top of the list. The enrichment was not an artifact of one run: across all four strict (same-run decoder, same-run embedding) pairs available, it held at every seed at λ = 2 (two to three marker hits each, including both gate-passing seeds; p = 0.0014–0.022, with KRT5, KRT6A, SPRR2B, SPRR3 among the surviving keratinization genes) and was *strongest* at the maximal-dose recovery point (λ = 5), where six markers were hit — including CDKN2A itself, the canonical HPV+ driver readout (p < 0.001; Table S7). The surviving program is consistent with the canonical HPV-status contrast reported for HNSCC[12] (CDKN2A/p16-axis cell-cycle entry and keratinizing differentiation), recovered *from the post-repair representation alone*. The program that survives is not merely "whatever the model can still fit"; it is the disease program the field already believes in, and it is most legible exactly where the repair does its job best. We position this as a demonstration (C3), not a discovery: it closes the loop by showing what audited repair preserves at the biological level.

### Repair preserves signal share but does not improve a downstream clinical endpoint

The transferable-fraction statistic R reports what share of a run's in-sample fit crosses donors; it does not by itself say whether a downstream clinical task improves. We therefore evaluated the external cohort's own etiological endpoint directly — leave-one-donor-out classification of HPV status from slice-mean embeddings with a closed-form ridge probe (Table S9). At λ = 0 the endpoint was already well above chance (LODO AUROC 0.906 ± 0.063 across the three seeds; all three seeds sat above the LDI gate, at 1.88–2.49), so the pre-repair model was not merely memorizing identity — the association it fit was largely donor-transferable, consistent with the cohort sitting just below the Proposition 1 threshold. Under repair the endpoint did not improve: every λ > 0 gave a lower mean LODO AUROC than baseline (λ = 0.5, 0.781 ± 0.072; λ = 1, 0.766 ± 0.098; λ = 2, 0.813 ± 0.041; λ = 5, 0.766 ± 0.189), a drop of ≈ 0.09–0.14, present in all three seeds at λ = 0.5 and consistent in direction across doses. This is the **fidelity cost of repair**, and it is the expected outcome on this cohort rather than a defect of the operator: with 16 patients and one slide per patient, the slice-level LOO and the donor-level LDO statistics coincide by construction, so there is no sibling-slice leakage channel for repair to remove (the sibling-slice inflation effect of the previous section is therefore absent here). Repair's benefit is conditional on a leakage channel existing; its cost — a partial erasure of the transferable signal, the same effect that sets R = 0.38–0.59 at accepted cells — is paid regardless. The λ = 5 seed spread (0.563–0.938, SD 0.189, versus SD 0.041 at λ = 2) further shows that strong repair is seed-unstable at this donor count, matching the n\* ≈ 16–32 boundary. We report the endpoint as a negative result and read it as a scope condition: on a one-slide-per-donor cohort the audit already certifies donor-transferable signal, and repair should be reserved for cohorts in which a leakage channel — multiple slides per donor, or batch–label coupling — actually exists.

## DISCUSSION

MRP-ST-Repair is a training-time method that removes patient-identity leakage from ST models while keeping the transferable disease signal, and its design produced four findings we consider general.

First, **the method is measured on both removal and preservation.** Post-hoc cleanup is provably self-defeating for the strong-signal regime (the subspace argument of Figure 1B), so the repair is built into training; the same calibrated instrument used for detection is then reused as an acceptance referee, and it stays non-blind across the whole repair family (75/75 positive-control and 45/45 confounding-control verdicts; Table 1).

Second, **there is a theory of when acceptance is even possible.** Proposition 1 shows that a naive "remove-and-preserve" dual gate is structurally empty whenever the pre-repair association is large relative to the null scale (τ_base/σ̂ ≳ 2.35) — a condition that holds for most strong-signal cohorts but not for the one cohort where accepted operating points were observed. This converts acceptance from a hand-tuned threshold into a quantity that can be checked before training, and it motivates the three-axis protocol that replaces the dual gate.

Third, **repairability and evaluation hygiene are properties of cohort structure, not of method tuning.** The same operator, doses, seeds, and stability interventions produced opposite outcomes on two real cohorts, and the deciding variables were the label type (etiological vs. ordinal), the donor count relative to a measurable boundary, and the split level (the sibling-slice inflation effect means slice-level cross-validation silently reports re-identification, exactly when one checks whether repair worked). Before training, one can ask what kind of label one has, how many donors the signal needs to survive de-identification, and at what level to evaluate.

Fourth, **surviving signal is interpretable signal.** The keratinization enrichment means the acceptance protocol is not preserving an arbitrary residual; it is preserving the canonical HPV biology, which supports the use of the protocol as a discovery filter: what passes an audited repair is a candidate for cross-cohort transfer.

**Limitations.** (i) One external real cohort (n = 16, one slide per patient); the boundary is mapped on simulations with linear ridge probes as the readout — the subspace argument predicts backbone-agnosticity, and the GCN replication supports it, but other encoder families are untested. (ii) The repair family is adversarial; the contrastive arm (cross-patient positive pairs) remains a plausible alternative and was deliberately not developed here. (iii) Axis (ii) of acceptance — in-sample memorization clearance — was partial on the identity-confounding control (~12%), because memorization rides donor-specific components beyond the planted fingerprint; we report this as a bound on what in-sample metrics can certify rather than as a success — on an identity-confounding cohort with no true signal to clear, a partial clearance is the expected outcome, not a shortfall. (iv) The perfect-generalization point, observed in the non-hardened replication (τ at ceiling, with the LDI gate exceeded only by a rebound), invites interpretation, but at three seeds per dose we do not treat dose optima as precise quantities; we report ranges and seed spreads throughout. (v) The boundary n\* is defined for one signal-channel structure (shared program + donor fingerprint); cohorts with additional leakage channels (batch, platform) will need their own grids, which the released generator and trainer make a configuration change rather than a new study. (vi) The downstream clinical endpoint does not improve under repair on the external cohort (LODO HPV AUROC 0.906 at baseline vs. 0.766–0.813 under repair; Table S9): because this cohort has one slide per donor, there is no sibling-slice leakage to remove, so repair pays a pure fidelity cost. We state this explicitly as a boundary on clinical claims — repair removes leakage where a leakage channel exists, and its contribution on such a cohort is the audit-certified measurement of how much signal is donor-transferable, not a gain in downstream accuracy.

**Practical protocol.** For a small-cohort ST study: (1) audit with LDI before any modeling claims; (2) if leaking, do not attempt post-hoc cleanup — retrain with patient-adversarial repair; (3) check Proposition 1's ratio τ_base/σ̂ — if it exceeds ≈ 2.35, a dual "remove-and-preserve" gate is not the right acceptance, use the three-axis protocol; (4) verify at the donor level; (5) accept only cohorts with a donor budget above the feasibility boundary for their signal strength; (6) read surviving programs out through the decoder and check them against known biology. Every step is a script in the released repository.

## EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

### Resource availability

**Data.** The external cohort is GSE268014[13] (Visium, HPV+ / HPV− HNSCC; 16-patient balanced subset, 8 HPV+ and 8 HPV−, 57,872 spots, mean 3,617 spots/slide) from the Gene Expression Omnibus. The cervical cohort (35 Visium slides with ordinal severity labels) is as described in the companion study[1] (10x Genomics public datasets and GSE217237[14]). Simulated cohorts are generated by the released simulator with fixed seeds.

**Code.** All training, simulation, audit, aggregation, and analysis scripts, with exact seeds and configuration manifests, are released at [repository URL to be finalized before submission].

### Notation, probes, and the instrument

For a cohort of m slides (slice-mean embeddings after training), y ∈ {0,1}^m the label vector, and X the embedding matrix: **τ_in** is the in-sample Kendall association between the ridge-probe score X·w and y, with w fit on all m slides; **τ_LOO** is its leave-one-out counterpart (probe fit on m−1, scored on the held-out slide); **τ_LDO** is the donor-level variant in which all sibling slides of a donor leave jointly. The **label-dependence index** is LDI = (τ_LOO − τ⊥)/σ̂, where σ̂ is the standard deviation of the permutation null of τ_LOO (refit inference: the probe is refit per permutation) at B = 2,000 permutations, seed 20261010, and τ⊥ = 0.001 the null floor; the calibrated detection gate is LDI < 1.645 (one-sided 5%). The **analytic ceiling** τ_max for binary labels with class sizes (n₀, n₁) is n₀n₁ / sqrt((n₀n₁ + T)(n₀n₁)), where T = C(n₀,2)+C(n₁,2); e.g., 0.707 for (50,40), 0.713 for (18,14), 0.7303 for (8,8). Permutation-depth sensitivity: recomputing the external-cohort nulls at B = 10,000 moved σ̂ by ≤ 4.4% and flipped 0/15 gate decisions (Table S4).

### Proposition 1

Stated and proved in Results; the feasibility threshold is τ_base/σ̂ < 1.645/c (≈ 2.35 for c = 0.7). Observed ratios: shared-program simulation 5.8; cervical ≈ 2.4; HNSCC ≈ 2.2 (Table 1).

### Repair operator (Method B)

Encoder: two-layer graph attention (GAT; GCN variant in Table S5) over the spot-level kNN graph (k = 6, both directions; slide coordinates offset by 50,000 units per slide to prevent cross-slide edges); decoder: linear, reconstructing log1p library-normalized expression over the union panel (top-2,000-variance genes per sample, union across samples; 6,586 genes for the HNSCC cohort). Loss: reconstruction + λ · CE(adversary(GRL(z)), patient-ID), GRL reversal strength α = 0 during the warm-up window (W epochs), then α = min(1, 2·(epoch − W)/(epochs_total − W)) · α_cap with α_cap = 1; adversary: 2-layer MLP. The base configuration sets W = 0; the stability variant adds W = 50, gradient clipping at 1.0, and spectral normalization of the adversary. Optimization: Adam, 300 epochs, full batch. Doses λ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5}; three training seeds per cell (matches the simulation seed). Hardware: single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 SUPER GPU (32 GB) on a cloud instance (AutoDL), ~30 s per training run on the external cohort.

### Simulations

Generator (released): spots on a jittered hex grid (1,500/slice), sparse log-normal background expression with donor-level batch fingerprint N(0, 0.6) per gene, per-donor program response coefficients; shared-program arm with planted amplitude (0.5–2.5), identity-confounding arm with class-balanced fingerprints, weak-signal arm with heterogeneity (response range and dropout as in the companion study). Phase grid: donors n ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32, 64} (5:4 class ratio), k ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8} slices/donor, signals {1.0, 0.3}, donor-invariant parameters shared across a donor's slices; three seeds per cell (seeds 3–4 at boundary cells in Table S6). Cell statistics: mean R = τ_LOO/τ_in over seeds.

### C3-lite gene-direction analysis

On the four strict pairs available (λ = 2, seeds 0–2, and the λ = 5 maximal-dose seed; external cohort, stability variant): ridge fit of HPV direction d on slice-mean embeddings (α = 1, standardized); gene loadings L = W·d through each run's own decoder W (strict pairing); enrichment computed in unique-symbol space (6,586-entry panel → 4,647 unique symbols, per-gene max |loading| across duplicate entries), by hypergeometric test against the 11-marker in-panel background, with a 2,000-draw permutation null on random 100-gene sets; all four strict pairs in Table S7. Gene names normalized (platform prefix stripped).

### Statistical analysis

All reported gate decisions use the calibrated LDI with the seeds and permutation depths above; the transferable-fraction statistic is R = τ_LOO/τ_in within the same run (the donor-transferable share of what the run fits in-sample). Gate decisions on the external cohort were rechecked at B = 10,000 with no flips (Table S4); all other gate decisions use B = 2,000, and under the largest σ̂ perturbation observed in the recheck (4.4% on σ̂, relative to the B = 2,000 estimate; LDI rescales reciprocally, at most +4.6% in the worst direction) no reported decision changes — the closest-to-gate value outside those directly rechecked runs (identity-confounding maximum LDI = 1.55) would move to at most ≈ 1.62, still under the gate.
